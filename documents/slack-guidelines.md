# IGME-430 Slack

We use Slack for course communication and for getting help fast. This page covers signing in,
what the channels are for, and the ground rules. Read it once in week 1 and you should be set.

This workspace is part of the course, so everything in it falls under the RIT Student Code of
Conduct the same way the classroom does.

## Why Slack and not Discord

This course used Discord in past semesters, and most of you are more comfortable there. Three
reasons we're on Slack instead:

1. **Coursework privacy.** RIT holds course communication to a standard that the enterprise
   tools meet and Discord does not.
2. **Industry uses it.** Companies rarely run internal communication on Discord. You are one or
   two years from a job where Slack is where work happens, so the practice is worth something.
3. **Boundaries.** When the same app is your social life and your coursework, both get worse.

## Signing in

**Start here: https://rit.enterprise.slack.com/**

Getting to that exact URL is most of the battle. You'll see a green **Sign in with RIT login**
button. Click it and you'll land on RIT's normal authentication screen, the same one you see
everywhere else. Sign in with your username (`abc1234`) and your RIT password.

> ⚠️ **You are not creating a Slack account.** There is no Slack username and no Slack password.
> If a screen is asking you to type an email address or make up a password, you're in the wrong
> place. Go back to the URL above and look for the green button.

You don't need to type `@rit.edu` or `@g.rit.edu` anywhere, which is the part that catches
people. A personal Google or Slack account won't get you in either.

Once you're through, that page lists every RIT workspace you belong to. You should see our course
workspace. **If you can't find it or can't get in, tell me right away** so I can add you. Don't
wait until the night you need something.

Your display name comes from the RIT directory and can't be changed in Slack. To change it, go
to start.rit.edu → Directory Information → Name Preferences. That updates Slack, Zoom, and
myCourses together.

**Worth doing:** add a profile picture, and get the phone app. I'm generally against installing
an app for something that works fine in a browser, and the Slack mobile app is the exception I
make. It's the fastest way to reach me when something comes up the night before a deadline.

### On your phone

The mobile app doesn't put an RIT option on its sign-in screen, and **Sign in with Google will not
get you in.** Use the workspace URL path instead:

1. Install Slack from the App Store or Google Play and open it.
2. Choose **Sign in with workspace URL**. On iOS it's hiding under "Having trouble?" on the email
   screen.
3. Enter `rit-community` as the workspace URL.
4. Tap **Sign In with RIT**, then your normal RIT username and password.

`rit-community` is the workspace that hands the app off to RIT authentication. Once you're through
it you're signed in to every RIT workspace you belong to, this course included.

**Already have Slack on your phone** for another RIT workspace? Swipe right for the channels menu,
tap the workspace icon at the top left, then **Add a workspace → Sign in to another workspace**,
and pick up from step 2.

RIT's official version of these steps is
[in the ITS knowledge base](https://help.rit.edu/sp?id=kb_article&table=kb_knowledge&sysparm_article=KB0043536).

## Ground rules

### 1. Be decent to each other

Disagree with ideas as much as you want. Do not go after people. If something here makes you
uncomfortable, message me directly and I will deal with it.

### 2. Ask course questions in public

Post in **#430-general** instead of DMing me. Someone else almost certainly has the same
question, and a public answer helps all of them at once.

Save DMs for things that are genuinely private: grades, personal situations, an emergency.

### 3. Reply in threads

Use **Reply in thread** instead of posting into the main channel. It keeps things readable when
thirty people are talking.

### 4. Ask questions that can actually be answered

"It's not working" cannot be answered. Say what you were trying to do, what you expected, what
happened instead, and what you already tried. Paste the actual error, not a description of it.

This matters more in 430 than in most classes. Server code fails in layers, and the useful
information is usually in a stack trace or a status code rather than in what you saw on screen.
Learning to write a good bug report is a real professional skill and you are practicing it here.

**Use code blocks.** Wrap code in triple backticks so it stays readable. For anything longer
than a screen, use a snippet: the **+** next to the message box, then **Create a text snippet**.

### 5. Help each other, but do not hand over your work

Helping is expected: explain a concept in your own words, point someone at the documentation
they missed, talk through their logic, tell them what fixed the same error for you.

Pasting your working file so someone can copy it is not helping. Neither is writing their route
handler for them.

**The test: after you help, could they do it again on their own?** If yes, you helped. If no, you
did it for them, and you have both now got a problem under the academic honesty policy.

### 6. Do not paste AI-generated code into this workspace

Read the AI policy in the syllabus carefully, because this course is stricter than some of your
others. Using AI to generate code for an assignment is an academic honesty violation here.

That extends to Slack. If you drop AI-generated code into **#430-general** to help someone, you
have handed a classmate a policy violation they didn't ask for, and they may not know it. Don't
do it.

You can absolutely use these tools to understand concepts, plan, and debug. If an explanation you
post came from one, say so, the same way you'd cite where you found anything else.

### 7. Other people's work stays where they put it

Screenshots of a classmate's project or code don't get reposted without their okay.

### 8. Extensions go in #430-extension-requests, not DM

     late-homework language, this section changes with it. -->

**Don't write me a message. Use the workflow in the channel.** Open
**#430-extension-requests** and click the workflow button at the top. It asks a few questions and
sends the request straight to me, which means it lands in one place and I can actually track it.

**Use it for homework and for projects.** Things come up. If you get in touch before the deadline
we can usually work something out, and that is true for both.

**Before the deadline is the whole thing.** Something outside your control that you flag on
Tuesday is a conversation. The same thing raised Friday after the due date is much harder for me
to do anything about, and at that point the standard late penalties are what apply.

Two deadlines I can't move no matter how early you ask: **the final project**, which is a grading
deadline and not mine to give away, and anything we're reviewing together in the next class,
where being without your work defeats the point of the session.

If something serious is going on in your life, DM me instead and as early as you can. Things
happen. It just needs to be a conversation before the deadline rather than after it.

### 9. #430-random is yours, within reason

Memes, side projects, job postings, whatever. It's meant to be fun and I'm not going to police
it closely.

The one line: keep it something you'd be comfortable with the whole class seeing, because the
whole class is seeing it. Nothing that targets a person or a group, and nothing that would be a
problem under the Code of Conduct. If you're weighing whether something clears that bar, it
probably doesn't.

### 10. I am not on Slack at 3 AM

Message whenever you want, just don't expect a reply outside working hours. Your odds are much
better here than over email. I read Slack on weekdays and usually reply the same day.
